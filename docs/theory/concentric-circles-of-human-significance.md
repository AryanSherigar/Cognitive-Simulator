# The Concentric Circles of Human Significance

Status: author's working framework, v1.0 (shared 2026-11-25). **Planned for integration into
CogSim before v0.2.** Not yet implemented. Part A is the author's theory, verbatim. Part B is the
integration recommendation.

## Part A — The theory (verbatim)

### 1. Introduction

The Concentric Circles of Human Significance is a theory that attempts to explain how individuals assign different levels of significance to other human beings, how those hierarchies influence their decisions, and how these individual hierarchies contribute to larger societal phenomena such as favouritism, prejudice, discrimination, and conflict.

The theory proposes that every individual has a subjective hierarchy of human significance, represented by concentric circles organised around their self-interest.

The fundamental idea is that not all human beings have equal significance to an individual, even when that individual believes that all human beings are morally equal.

The theory distinguishes between two important elements:

1. Self-interest and concentric circles: Determine how significant different people are to an individual.
2. Moral baseline: Determines the individual's underlying principles about how people ought to be treated.

These two elements interact and can reinforce or contradict each other.

### 2. Self-interest

#### 2.1 Definition

Self-interest is the preservation and fulfilment of an individual's mind and life.

It encompasses both conscious and subconscious needs, desires, attachments, values, and motivations.

Self-interest is not restricted to physical survival, financial benefits, material possessions, or personal comfort.

It can include:

- Physical preservation and well-being.
- Mental and emotional preservation.
- Personal goals and ambitions.
- Identity, values, and beliefs.
- Relationships and attachments.
- The existence and well-being of people who are deeply significant to the individual.
- Things whose loss or fulfilment substantially affects the individual's psychological existence.

For example, a mother might consider her child's survival more important than her own. She might sacrifice her life to save the child.

This does not contradict the theory because the child's existence may have become more significant to the mother's self-interest than her own physical survival.

#### 2.2 Conscious and subconscious self-interest

Self-interest includes both conscious and subconscious influences.

An individual may consciously understand what they want, while subconscious attachments, fears, motivations, and psychological needs influence their decisions without their awareness.

Consequently, an individual may not accurately understand why they behave in a particular way.

#### 2.3 Self-interest is dynamic

Self-interest is not permanent.

It can change as an individual's:

- Goals and ambitions change.
- Values and beliefs evolve.
- Relationships develop or deteriorate.
- Experiences reshape their priorities.
- Psychological needs and attachments change.

Something that once mattered enormously to someone can eventually become irrelevant to their existence.

#### 2.4 Actions can contradict self-interest

An individual can act against their own self-interest.

For example, someone might:

- Pursue an objective that ultimately damages their mental health.
- Remain in a destructive relationship.
- Sacrifice their well-being to satisfy another person's expectations.
- Make decisions based on subconscious motivations that work against their actual interests.

Therefore, self-interest describes what matters to the individual, not necessarily what every action achieves.

### 3. Concentric circles of human significance

#### 3.1 Definition

The concentric circles represent an individual's subjective hierarchy of human significance.

Each individual has their own hierarchy, organised around their self-interest.

The position of a person within this hierarchy determines their relative significance and the degree to which their interests influence the individual's self-interest.

The closer someone is to the innermost circle, the greater their preferential consideration.

#### 3.2 The individual is not automatically the centre of importance

Although self-interest is at the centre of the structure, the individual themselves does not automatically occupy the highest circle.

Other people can occupy a higher position than the individual.

For example:

- A mother may place her child above herself.
- Someone may place their parents above their own ambitions.
- Someone may sacrifice their own well-being for a person they consider more significant.

The individual can occupy different positions in their own hierarchy.

#### 3.3 Human significance is not equivalent to affection

A person's significance does not necessarily indicate that they are loved or positively valued.

Someone can be highly significant because of:

- Love.
- Admiration.
- Dependency.
- Fear.
- Hatred.
- Trauma.
- Resentment.
- Emotional attachment.

For example, an abusive partner may occupy someone's innermost circle despite causing them considerable harm.

Similarly, someone can hate another person while that person remains extremely significant to their psychological existence.

### 4. Classification of circles

The theory has four broad classifications of human significance.

| Region | Significance | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Inner circles | Highest personal significance | People who receive the greatest preferential consideration. Their interests can strongly influence the individual's self-interest, sometimes overriding the individual's usual moral standards. |
| 2. Community / group circles | Shared identity and belonging | People who matter because of their association with a community, group, identity, or collective. They may receive preferential treatment over people outside that group. |
| 3. Indifference circles | Little personal significance | People who have little influence over the individual's self-interest. The individual may not feel strongly motivated to help or harm them. Their moral baseline becomes particularly relevant to how they are treated. |
| 4. Hostility circles | Negative significance | People towards whom the individual has active negative bias, prejudice, resentment, or hostility. The individual may oppose their interests even when their moral baseline would otherwise support helping them. |

These are broad regions, not four individual circles. Each region can contain numerous circles and finer distinctions.

#### 4.1 No fixed number of circles

There is no predetermined number of circles.

An individual can have thousands of circles.

- A circle can contain one person.
- A circle can contain multiple people.
- People can move between circles.
- Boundaries are not rigid.
- The structure can change completely over time.

#### 4.2 Membership is not determined by social categories

Circle membership is subjective.

It is not automatically determined by:

- Family relationships.
- Friendship.
- Nationality.
- Religion.
- Race.
- Social status.
- Political affiliation.

A stranger can occupy someone's innermost circle, while a family member can occupy an outer circle.

A person from a group that someone dislikes can also occupy their innermost circle.

#### 4.3 Indifference and hostility are different

Indifference does not mean someone is necessarily treated badly.

A person in the indifference region may receive fair treatment because of the individual's moral baseline, even if that person has little personal significance.

Hostility, on the other hand, involves active negative significance and can produce opposition or discriminatory treatment.

### 5. Inheritance and development of circles

One of the central propositions of the theory is:

> Circles are mostly inherited, whereas morals are cultivated.

#### 5.1 How circles are inherited

The initial hierarchy of human significance is shaped by:

- Family.
- Upbringing.
- Culture.
- Environment.
- Social conditioning.
- Group identity.
- Early experiences.

For example, a child might inherit hostility towards a particular religious or ethnic community without ever having personally interacted with its members.

The Nazi persecution of Jewish people illustrates how group hostility can be cultivated through upbringing, ideology, propaganda, and social conditioning.

#### 5.2 Circles are dynamic

Although circles are mostly inherited, they are not permanent.

A person can move:

- Inwards, becoming more significant.
- Outwards, becoming less significant.
- From a positive significance position to a negative one.
- From hostility towards indifference or attachment.

There is no universal rule governing these movements. They depend on individual psychology, experiences, relationships, and circumstances.

#### 5.3 Conscious and unconscious circle placement

An individual may not accurately understand their own hierarchy.

There can be a difference between:

1. Where an individual believes someone is positioned.
2. Where that person is actually positioned.
3. How the individual behaves towards them.

For example, a parent might genuinely believe their child is their highest priority, while repeatedly demonstrating that personal ambition takes precedence.

The individual may rationalise their decisions and convince themselves that their actual priorities are different.

#### 5.4 The uniqueness hypothesis

The theory currently contains an unproven proposition:

> No two people can occupy exactly the same position in an individual's hierarchy of significance.

The reasoning is that:

- Every individual has a unique psychological composition.
- Every relationship affects an individual differently.
- Consequently, even people who appear equally significant might have different degrees of influence over the individual's self-interest.

However, this is currently an intuition rather than a demonstrated property.

Multiple people can occupy the same broad circle, even if their exact positions within that circle are different.

### 6. Morals and moral baseline

#### 6.1 Morals

Morals are principles and standards an individual acquires that determine what they consider right, wrong, acceptable, or unacceptable.

Unlike circles, which are mostly inherited, morals are cultivated through:

- Education.
- Personal experiences.
- Observation.
- Reflection.
- Social interaction.
- Learning.
- Changes in understanding.

Morals can change dramatically over an individual's lifetime.

#### 6.2 Moral baseline

The moral baseline is the individual's underlying moral standard for how people ought to be treated, without additional preferential treatment or negative bias arising from their circle positions.

It acts as a reference point for understanding the interaction between personal significance and moral principles.

For example: an individual genuinely believes all humans deserve equal consideration. However, they consistently favour their own community over strangers. Their moral baseline might remain universal, while their circles influence how they actually treat people.

#### 6.3 Belief and action

The theory introduces a particular definition of belief:

> Belief is understood through how an individual acts when their life directly depends on the particular thing.

This distinction is intended to separate what someone declares from what their actions reveal under fundamental circumstances.

Three elements must remain distinct:

- What someone professes to believe.
- Their underlying moral baseline.
- Their actual behaviour when circles and circumstances influence their decisions.

A single action does not necessarily demonstrate that a person's moral baseline has changed.

For example, a parent stealing food to save their child does not automatically mean they have stopped believing stealing is wrong. The child's position in their hierarchy might override their usual moral standard.

### 7. Interaction between circles and morals

This is one of the most important mechanisms in the theory.

Circles and morals are distinct variables that influence human behaviour.

| Concentric circles | Moral baseline |
| --- | --- |
| Mostly inherited | Cultivated |
| Determine personal significance | Determine moral standards |
| Influence preferential treatment | Establish acceptable treatment |
| Can operate unconsciously | Can operate consciously or unconsciously |
| Can change over time | Can change over time |

They can interact in several ways.

#### 7.1 Circles override morals

An individual believes all humans are equal but gives preferential treatment to their own community. Their circle structure influences their decisions despite their universal moral baseline.

#### 7.2 Morals override circle preferences

An individual feels little personal attachment to a stranger but helps them because they believe helping is morally right.

#### 7.3 Circles and morals reinforce each other

An individual favours their own group and also believes that group deserves preferential treatment.

#### 7.4 Neither provides a decisive answer

There can be situations where neither circle placement nor moral principles distinguish between available choices. The eventual decision may depend on circumstances or chance.

#### 7.5 The independence of circles and morals

Circles and morals can change independently. There are four possibilities:

1. Circles change, morals remain the same.
2. Morals change, circles remain the same.
3. Both change.
4. Neither changes.

### 8. The role of self-interest in human decisions

The theory also contains a broader proposition about human motivation.

> Every motivated human action ultimately originates from or serves the individual's self-interest.

This is a form of psychological egoism.

The proposition relies on the broad definition of self-interest, including conscious and subconscious needs, desires, attachments, values, and motivations.

Examples:

- A parent sacrifices themselves for their child because the child's survival matters more to them than their own survival.
- Someone saves a stranger because witnessing their death would cause personal distress or violate their values.
- Someone sacrifices their comfort for their parents because their parents occupy a more significant position in their hierarchy.

However, the theory allows individuals to act against their actual self-interest.

It also allows for chance when there is no distinguishing motivation, circle influence, or moral preference between available outcomes.

This proposition remains unproven and requires further philosophical examination.

### 9. How circle placement influences decisions

Circle placement determines how strongly another person's interests influence an individual's self-interest.

However, it does not automatically determine the priority of every activity involving that person.

**Example: a parent misses their child's birthday for a business meeting.** Suppose a parent misses their child's birthday to attend an extremely important business meeting. The child occupies the innermost circle, while the business associate occupies a more distant circle.

This does not necessarily mean the business associate has become more significant. The business opportunity might:

- Secure the child's financial future.
- Protect the family's financial position.
- Fulfil an ambition that matters to the parent.

The opportunity can derive its significance partly from the interests of someone occupying a closer circle.

Alternatively, if the parent chooses personal prestige over the child's well-being, this could indicate that the parent themselves occupies a higher position than the child, or that the child's actual position was never as close as the parent believed.

The significance of a person and the significance of an opportunity are therefore distinct.

### 10. Examples that illustrate the theory

| Example | Explanation within the theory |
| --- | --- |
| Mother sacrifices her life for her child | The child occupies a higher position in her hierarchy than herself. |
| Someone favours their own community | Community members occupy closer circles than outsiders. |
| A person helps a stranger despite indifference | Their moral baseline motivates the action. |
| Someone discriminates against a group they have never met | Hostility can be inherited through upbringing and social conditioning. |
| Someone loves an abusive partner | Significance does not necessarily mean positive influence. |
| A person claims to prioritise their child but repeatedly chooses personal prestige | Their perceived hierarchy may differ from their actual hierarchy. |
| Someone misses a child's birthday for a business opportunity that secures the child's future | The opportunity derives significance partly from the child's position in the hierarchy. |
| Someone opposes helping a distant population despite believing in human equality | Their circle structure and moral baseline may be in conflict, or their professed moral principle may not reflect their actual baseline. |

### 11. The theory's primary objectives

The theory aims to explain two broad phenomena.

**A. Distribution of human significance**

- Why do individuals assign different degrees of significance to different people?
- Why do some people receive preferential treatment?
- Why can someone be more important than the individual themselves?
- Why can people be significant despite being hated or feared?
- How do inherited and cultivated experiences shape these hierarchies?

**B. Societal consequences**

- Why do people favour their own communities?
- How does inherited prejudice develop?
- Why can universal moral beliefs coexist with discriminatory behaviour?
- How do individual hierarchies contribute to collective discrimination?
- How can individual preferences accumulate into larger societal conflicts?

### 13. Current theoretical foundation

1. Self-interest is the preservation and fulfilment of an individual's mind and life.
2. Self-interest includes conscious and subconscious influences.
3. Self-interest changes over time.
4. Individuals can act against their own self-interest.
5. Every individual has a subjective hierarchy of human significance.
6. This hierarchy is represented through concentric circles.
7. Individuals are not automatically the highest priority in their own hierarchy.
8. Circles are mostly inherited, whereas morals are cultivated.
9. Circle positions are dynamic and can change.
10. Circle positions can be consciously misunderstood.
11. Moral baselines and circles are independent but interacting variables.
12. Circle placement influences preferential treatment.
13. Moral baselines provide a standard for acceptable treatment.
14. Indifference and hostility are distinct forms of significance.
15. Individual hierarchies can help explain broader societal prejudice and discrimination.

**The central proposition:** Human beings do not distribute significance equally among other human beings. Each individual possesses a subjective, dynamic hierarchy of human significance, organised around their self-interest. This hierarchy influences how much preferential consideration different people receive. Although individuals cultivate moral principles about how humans ought to be treated, their inherited and evolving circles of significance can reinforce, contradict, or override those principles. The interaction between these two forces shapes individual behaviour and contributes to broader societal patterns of favouritism, prejudice, and discrimination.

## Part B — Integration recommendation for CogSim

### B.1 Verdict

**Adopt it.** It supplies the persona variable CogSim is missing. Today a persona's decision
disposition is essentially one number (`authority_deference_prior`). Every gate scenario,
however, is a conflict between parties:

| Gate | Parties in conflict |
| --- | --- |
| 1 / 2 / 3 | supervisor vs. three tagged-out technicians |
| 4 | trapped technician vs. hospital patients on the grid |
| 5a | own career vs. firm / clients / law |
| 5 | own record vs. hospital and households |

"Who matters to this person, and how much" is exactly the variable that should separate one
persona's choice from another's.

### B.2 Mapping onto the architecture

| Theory | v0.1 | v1.0 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circles (mostly inherited, can be unconscious) | New persona field consumed by the subcortical drive weighting | Evolution Model priors plus upbringing episodes in the developmental curriculum |
| Moral baseline (cultivated, conscious) | Biography prose (prompt-safe) | Knowledge Model weights (books, reflection, salience-weighted loss) |
| Hostility = negative significance | Signed significance values | Same |
| Professed vs. actual belief | `external_speech` and consolidation narrative vs. `physical_action` | Same, plus reconstructive memory |
| Bridge "social concept → survival trigger" | Appraisal reports who is at stake; drives weight threat by significance | Learned bridge |

### B.3 Proposed implementation (before v0.2)

1. **Persona schema:** `significance: {role_or_group: float in [-1, 1]}` (including `self`), and
   `moral_baseline: {principle: strength}`, kept as separate fields because the theory treats
   them as independent variables.
2. **Mechanism, never prompt text.** This is the same rule as the existing no-numeric-leak test:
   significance numbers must not appear in prompts. Extend the appraisal with a per-party stake
   (who is threatened, how badly). The drive core then weights threat to each party by that
   party's significance, so an inner-circle party at risk raises arousal mechanically.
3. **Each scenario declares its parties** (as it already declares its `actions`), so stakes and
   significance can be matched.
4. **New gate: circles vs. morals under load.** Use dilemmas where the circle-favoured option and
   the moral-baseline-favoured option differ (one inner-circle person vs. several strangers;
   in-group vs. out-group with equal stakes). Run them at rest, under chronic load (Gate 5
   throttling) and under `sam_hpa_bypass`.
   - **Prediction derived from the theory and the architecture:** as compute is throttled,
     choices shift from baseline-driven to circle-driven; the shift is absent under the bypass
     control.
   - This is the first experiment in which the theory can be confirmed or refuted by the
     simulator.
5. **Measurement route for open questions 2 and 4.** Infer circle positions from forced
   trade-offs (revealed preference), then compare them with the persona's stated priorities and
   with its post-hoc consolidation narrative.

### B.4 Cautions

- **Do not encode §8 (psychological egoism).** Once self-interest includes values, attachments
  and others' welfare, every action qualifies by definition. The proposition then cannot be
  falsified and predicts nothing. This is the classic objection to psychological egoism (e.g.
  Butler). Nothing operational depends on it.
- **§5.4 (uniqueness).** In a model, significance is continuous, so exact ties essentially never
  occur. Treat it as a modelling convenience, not an empirical claim to defend.
- **Position against related work** when writing it up:
  - Hierocles' Stoic concentric circles
  - Singer, *The Expanding Circle*
  - research on moral-circle size / moral expansiveness
  - social identity theory (Tajfel)
  - parochial altruism and kin selection
  - dual-process moral judgment (Greene)

  The theory's distinctive contributions appear to be:
  - significance and the moral baseline as two independent, interacting variables
  - hostility as negative significance, distinct from indifference
  - significance ≠ affection
  - the self not automatically at the centre
- **No tuning toward expected results.** The project's existing rule applies: the circles-vs-morals
  prediction is a hypothesis to test. Do not adjust significance weights until it comes true.
